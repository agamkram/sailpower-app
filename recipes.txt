WindCart model notes
Wind 10 m/s, sail 5x2 m, 24 kg chassis plus a 16 kg sail, rail 1000 N,
track 20 m. Figures are three steady cycles after the first, averaged over the
whole cycle including time at rest.

The rotation plan
-----------------
The six named recipes are gone. They were never six rotation rules: they mixed
three knobs at once, and the one that won on paper was another recipe at a
different cruise speed with identical turn timing. Three of them were also
handed a widened braking zone so they had room to turn in, so "turn two
seconds early" never meant that.

A plan is now two numbers, and cruise speed is a spec, not part of the plan.

  turn      how long the 90 degree slew takes
  turnLead  where that slew sits relative to arrival, as a fraction of its own
            length
            0   the slew begins once the cart is stopped
            1   the slew finishes exactly as the cart stops

turnLead was seconds at first, and that was wrong. The cart cruises out at
2.4 m/s and comes home at 8, so the same number of seconds is a short distance
one way and a long one the other. A one second lead left the sail wide open
four metres from home with the motor still hauling against it, and the setting
that was meant to read "slightly early" collapsed to five watts. Tied to the
turn instead, it means the same thing in both directions and cannot ask for the
sail to be open before the cart has arrived.

Braking starts where the stopping distance says it must. The lead reaches back
into the cruise, so a long lead really does turn the sail while it is still
making power, and the numbers show what that costs.

The sail angle is a command, not a commitment. Inside the lead it drives toward
the angle the stroke ends on, outside it drives back to the angle the stroke
runs at. A one-way turn meant a sail that opened too early kept opening while
the wind drove the cart backwards, until it sat pinned against the far cap half
open with nothing left to try.

Braking only promises to stop the cart, not to stop it at the cap, and in a
strong wind it stops short. The last stretch into each cap is closed at a creep
speed instead.

Presets vary turnLead alone, so the selector compares turn timing and nothing
else. Everything else is a spec slider.

Sliders that fit each other
---------------------------
The sliders were ranged one at a time, so they did not describe one machine.
Rail force stopped at 3000 N while the biggest sail in the strongest wind
pushes about 17600, which is how a wide sail came to be braked by the end stop
instead of the coils. Turn went down to 0.2 s for every sail, and an 8 m plate
needs 53 kN m to move that fast. Return speed went to 16 m/s with no regard
for motoring force, which droops with back-EMF and is gone by 22 m/s.

The sail and the wind are free. The drive is sized to them, so the three drive
sliders move their own ends:

  rail force  from what holds the sail standing still, to three times that.
              Also never below what shifts the all-up mass at 1 m/s2, or a
              tiny sail in light air got a rail of two newtons
  turn        no quicker than the slew drive can manage at 150 N m per m2 of
              sail. That works out at 0.075 s per metre of width whatever the
              drive is sized to, so 0.15 s for a 2 m sail and 0.6 s for an 8 m
  return      no faster than the coils can still hold against the edge-on drag
              with most of the force left over as margin

Best only offers plans inside those ends. scripts/range-check.mjs replays what
a range input does to its own value when the ends move, and checks that what
is left is still a machine that completes a cycle.

The cycle budget is what the sim will wait for before calling a plan no plan.
It was a flat forty seconds, which reported a 40 m track crossed at a crawl as
impossible rather than as bad. It now follows the track and the speeds, capped
at 400 s. Searching uses one cycle at a 0.05 s step, which ranks candidates to
within a watt of the full run and keeps Best to a few seconds on the slowest
machine in the space.

The cart is captive: the bogie grips the rail from above, below and both
sides, so the overturning moment from the sail is a guide-wheel load and not a
tipping failure. At the default it is about eleven times what the weight alone
would resist. That load is still not charged for.

Forces and losses
-----------------
Carried:

  aero        flat plate, cos^2 law on yaw, plus edge and skin terms
  Cd          Hoerner's fit, 1.1 + 0.02(AR + 1/AR). A square plate is the
              bluffest at 1.14, slenderness lifts it to 1.30 by AR 10. The Cd
              spec is a trim around that, not the value
  yaw inertia m*w^2/12 about the vertical pivot, width squared
  added air   rho*(pi/4)*c^2*b with a slenderness factor, acting as cos^2 of
              yaw. Conservative, so it moves timing and peak force, not the watt-seconds
  slew work   spin-up through the motor, aero torque against the loaded plate,
              and no recovery on spin-down because a geared slew drive is not
              back-drivable. slewRegen buys that back if the drive is direct
  droop       motoring force falls as back-EMF rises and reaches zero at
              vNoLoad. Braking is thermal only, so it keeps the full 1000 N
  copper      cuMax*(F/fMax)^2 at every speed, not only while parked
  iron        ironRef*(v/10)^2
  rolling     crr against weight plus the sail thrust reacted through the
              guide rollers, which at 10 m/s is about twice the weight
  end stops   watt-seconds absorbed by a stop are charged, not deleted
  feathering  a stopping aid, not a cruise setting. Out on the power stroke a
              sail that out-pushes the coils just drives the cart faster until
              the forces balance, so the sail only sheds load while braking
              and turning at the far cap, where authority is really at stake

Budget at the default, in watts:

  made          705
  motor home   -144
  slew drive    -47
  copper+iron   -26
  end stops      -3
  net           484     cycle 11.8 s

Not carried: wind shear and gusts, unsteady aerodynamics beyond added mass,
side force and the overturning moment on the guide wheels, sail flex, thermal
derating, and any cost for the structure itself.

Sail shape
----------
The model used to see area and nothing else, so it could not tell a 5x2 sail
from a 2x5. At a constant 10 m2, with the plan solved for each shape:

  sail        AR     Cd   I kg m2   turn s   net W
  1.00x10.00 10.0  1.302      1.3     0.20     618
  2.00x5.00   2.5  1.158      5.3     0.30     530
  3.16x3.16   1.0  1.140     13.3     0.40     504
  5.00x2.00   2.5  1.158     33.3     0.70     487
  10.00x1.00 10.0  1.302    133.3     1.00     483

1x10 and 10x1 have identical area and identical Cd and differ by 135 W. All of
it is yaw inertia. Tall and narrow wins twice over: it is the bluffer shape and
it is cheap to turn. The best slew time tracks inertia, from 0.20 s for the
slender sail to 1.00 s for the wide one.

Cruise speed
------------
The optimum outbound is about 0.24 of wind speed, not the one third the old
defaults used. Drag-device theory puts the peak at U/3 for the power stroke
alone, but the return trip and the time at rest are fixed overhead, so a slower
outbound spreads that overhead across more generating time. It moves with the
other specs, which is why Solve exists rather than a hardcoded number.

Above rated wind
----------------
A 10 m2 sail out-pushes a 1000 N rail at about 12 m/s. That alone costs
nothing on the power stroke, because the surplus just accelerates the cart
until the forces balance. At the far cap the surplus is a harder stop, and
those watt-seconds are charged. The sail is not turned to shed it. Turning
it early made sail width start a second rotation.

On the way home the arrival slew stops at the angle the motor can still creep
against. Opening past that used to blow the cart back down the track, and the
turn was then commanded shut, so the last stretch home was taken edge-on at
about 0.05 m/s. That is what cut "while slowing" from about 2200 W to about
900 W above 24 m/s, and "done on arrival" with it. The rest of the turn now
happens at the cap. At 30 m/s the four presets are 2139, 2261, 2346 and 2164 W.
Opening earlier still costs motor work. It no longer stalls the cycle.

Sanity
------
Cp never exceeds the 4/27*Cd drag-device ceiling anywhere in the config space.
The best case found is 56% of it, at 8 m/s on a 40 m track with a 3000 N rail.

Across 1944 combinations of wind 4-30 m/s, sail 0.5-32 m2, track 8-40 m, rail
300-3000 N and all four rotation presets, every one finishes a cycle. A rail
that can hold the sail at rest always arrives under control. Where the sail
out-pushes the rail at rest, feathering still saves most stops, because the
cart is already moving and the apparent wind has dropped. 281 of them are not
controlled arrivals: the end stop absorbs the hit and those watt-seconds are charged.

The four corners that used to time out were one machine at each rotation
preset. A 2 m2 sail in a 14 m/s wind on a 40 m track with a 300 N rail pushes
280 N face-on, so the rail can hold it. Spare brake after feathering to 90%
is 30 N. A floor at 40 N had called that no stop at all, the cart crept the
whole track, and the sampler gave up at 160 s. Thirty newtons stops that cart
in a few metres. The cycle takes about 19 s.

Every commanded rotation takes exactly the turn time. Sail movement that looks
slower than that is feathering, which is continuous by nature.
